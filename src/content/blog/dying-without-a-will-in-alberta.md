Many people think their spouse gets everything if they die without a will. Sometimes that is true, but often it is not, and the difference can run into six figures.

## What Intestacy Actually Means

You die intestate if you have no will, or if your will fails (improperly signed, revoked, or no longer capable of distributing your estate). Part 3 of the *Wills and Succession Act*, SA 2010, c W-12.2 then decides how your property is distributed. The rules are in sections 58 to 70.

These rules send your estate to your family in a fixed order. The formula is not flexible, and the result depends on your family structure at the date of death.

## When the Spouse Gets Everything (and When They Do Not)

If you leave a spouse or adult interdependent partner (Alberta's term for a common-law partner), section 61 of the Act sets out two scenarios with very different outcomes.

**Scenario one: all your descendants (children, grandchildren, and so on) are also descendants of your surviving spouse or adult interdependent partner.** Your spouse or partner inherits the entire estate, and your children receive nothing directly. The rule assumes the surviving parent will provide for the shared children and later pass the estate on. Here, the common belief is correct.

**Scenario two: you have at least one descendant who is not also a descendant of your surviving spouse or partner.** Your spouse or partner receives a preferential share (the share the spouse takes first). It equals the greater of $150,000 or one-half of the net value of the estate. The rest is divided among your descendants.

Take a $1.2 million estate where you leave a spouse and one child from a prior relationship. Under scenario one, the whole estate would go to your spouse, but under scenario two, $600,000 goes to your spouse and $600,000 to your child.

If you or your partner has a child from before your current marriage, the intestacy rules will not distribute your estate the way you assume. For blended families, this is the main reason to have a will.

## What If There Are Both a Spouse and an Adult Interdependent Partner?

Under Alberta's *Adult Interdependent Relationships Act*, SA 2002, c A-4.5, you can be in an adult interdependent partnership while still legally married to someone else. You and your married spouse must have been living separate and apart (no longer living as a couple). So a person who separates without divorcing and finds a new partner may die with both a legal spouse and an adult interdependent partner.

Section 62 of the *Wills and Succession Act* covers this. The preferential share is split equally between the spouse and the adult interdependent partner, and the rest of the estate is distributed as usual. If there are no descendants, the estate is divided equally: one-half to the spouse and one-half to the adult interdependent partner.

But section 63 does disinherit a spouse in either of these situations:

- the spouse had been living separate and apart from the deceased (the person who died) for more than two years at the time of death; or
- there is a declaration of irreconcilability, a court order, or an agreement finalizing the marital affairs.

So if you have been separated for two years or less, with nothing formal in place, your spouse remains a beneficiary. Without a will, that spouse can still take a statutory share of your estate alongside your partner. A will can address this directly, but the intestacy rules cannot.

## No Spouse, No AIP, No Kids: What Then?

If you die without a spouse, adult interdependent partner, or descendants, the estate moves outward along family lines. Sections 66 and 67 use a parentelic system (a step-by-step search through family branches).

First, your estate goes to your parents. If only one survives, that parent takes it all, and if both have died, the estate passes to your siblings. If a sibling died before you and left children, those nieces and nephews take their parent's share by representation (in their parent's place).

If there are no surviving parents, siblings, nieces, or nephews, the estate splits in two: one-half to your mother's side and one-half to your father's side. Each half goes first to surviving grandparents and then to their descendants (your aunts, uncles, and cousins). The Act caps inheritance at the fourth degree of relationship (a count of family steps between relatives). If one side has no qualifying relatives, the other side takes the whole estate, and if neither side has any, the process repeats for the great-grandparent line.

If no one qualifies at any step, the estate escheats to the Crown (goes to the province) under the *Unclaimed Personal Property and Vested Property Act*, SA 2007, c U-1.5. To benefit a charity, friend, or cause instead, or to keep distant relatives from inheriting, you need a will.

## Who Gets to Administer the Estate

Without a will, the person who manages the estate is called an administrator. The court issues a grant of administration (court approval to manage the estate), not a grant of probate.

The *Estate Administration Act*, SA 2014, c E-12.5 sets the order of priority, from highest to lowest:

1. surviving spouse or adult interdependent partner
2. child of the deceased
3. grandchild
4. other descendant
5. parent
6. sibling
7. child of a sibling (if that person is a beneficiary under the intestacy)
8. other next of kin who are beneficiaries under the *Wills and Succession Act*

The person with highest priority can nominate someone else to act. In a blended family, the person with first priority to apply is often the one whose interests differ most from your children's.

## What an Intestacy Cannot Do

Even if the formula gives roughly the result you would have chosen, an intestacy can never:

- **Name a guardian for your minor children.** Only a will (or a separate appointment under the *Family Law Act*) can do that. Without one, guardianship is decided in court.
- **Leave anything to a stepchild.** Stepchildren are not descendants under the Act unless legally adopted.
- **Make charitable gifts.**
- **Leave specific items to specific people,** such as a family heirloom, a vehicle, or a particular piece of property.
- **State your wishes about funeral arrangements, cremation, or burial.**
- **Create a trust (property managed for someone else) for a minor or vulnerable beneficiary.** Funds inherited by a minor are held by the Public Trustee until age 18, and there is no structure to release them thoughtfully as the child matures.
