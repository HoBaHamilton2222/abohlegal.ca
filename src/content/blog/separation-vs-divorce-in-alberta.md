In law, separation and divorce are different. The difference affects your property, support, and parenting, and whether you can marry someone else.

## What Separation Actually Means

Separation is a fact, not a court case: nothing is filed, and there is no judge or certificate. You are separated when you and your spouse or partner live separate and apart, and at least one of you intends the relationship to be over. The Alberta government says plainly that there is no "legal separation" in Canada.

You can be separated while still living in the same home, as long as the relationship has really ended. Courts look at sleeping arrangements, finances, shared meals, social life as a couple, and how the household is run. Under s. 8(3) of the *Divorce Act*, RSC 1985, c 3 (2nd Supp), living together again for up to 90 days, mainly to reconcile, does not interrupt the separation period.

The date of separation still matters. It triggers:

- The one-year period for a no-fault divorce under the *Divorce Act*
- The start date for child support calculations in many cases
- The limitation period (the deadline to apply) under the *Family Property Act*, RSA 2000, c F-4.7
- The parenting status quo (the current arrangement) if the case goes to court
- Tax filing status, pension entitlements, and benefit designations

People often dispute the date later. Records such as an email saying "I am leaving", a new lease, a new address with Alberta Health, and closed joint bank accounts help prove it.

## What Divorce Actually Means

Divorce is a court order that ends a marriage, and only married spouses can get one. The *Divorce Act* is federal law and applies across Canada. In Alberta, divorces are granted by the Court of King's Bench.

Under s. 8 of the *Divorce Act*, a court grants a divorce only if the marriage has broken down. Breakdown is proved in one of three ways:

- The spouses have lived separate and apart for at least one year before the divorce is determined, and were living separate and apart when the proceeding started
- Adultery by the spouse being divorced
- Physical or mental cruelty by the spouse being divorced of a kind that makes continued cohabitation intolerable

Almost every Alberta divorce uses the one-year separation ground. It is no-fault, so you do not have to accuse your spouse of anything. Adultery and cruelty need evidence, add conflict, and do not change what you get.

You or your spouse must also have been habitually resident in Alberta for at least one year immediately before filing. Without that, Alberta has no jurisdiction (legal power) to grant you a divorce.

## If You Are Not Married

If you were never married, what matters is whether you are an adult interdependent partner (AIP) under the *Adult Interdependent Relationships Act*, SA 2002, c A-4.5. Section 3 sets out three ways to become AIPs:

- You have lived with the other person in a relationship of interdependence continuously for at least three years
- You have lived in a relationship of interdependence of some permanence and have a child together by birth or adoption
- You have signed a written Adult Interdependent Partner Agreement in the form set out by regulation

A relationship of interdependence does not have to be romantic or sexual. It covers two adults who share each other's lives, are emotionally committed, and function as an economic and domestic unit.

Until 2020, Alberta had no legislation for dividing property between unmarried partners. That changed when the *Matrimonial Property Act* was renamed the *Family Property Act* effective January 1, 2020, through Bill 28, the *Family Statutes Amendment Act, 2018*. The Act now applies to both married spouses and AIPs who separated on or after that date, and treats them similarly for property division. Separations before January 1, 2020 still fall under the old common-law principles (rules made by judges), such as unjust enrichment. These are harder to use and less predictable.

For unmarried parents, parenting is governed by Alberta's *Family Law Act*, SA 2003, c F-4.5. That Act also covers child support, partner support for former AIPs, guardianship, and parentage. Protection orders come from a separate statute, the *Protection Against Family Violence Act*, RSA 2000, c P-27. For married spouses who separate without divorcing, the *Family Law Act* applies to child support and parenting until a *Divorce Act* proceeding is started. ## What a Separation Agreement Can and Cannot Do

A separation agreement is a contract that sets out how you and your spouse or partner will handle the end of the relationship. It can be enforced like any other contract. Both of you need independent legal advice (advice from your own lawyer) and full financial disclosure, and the document should be signed, witnessed, and dated.

What a separation agreement can do:

- Divide property and debts, including the matrimonial home, vehicles, RRSPs, TFSAs, business interests, and pensions
- Set child support, spousal or partner support, and the terms for review or variation
- Set out parenting time, decision-making responsibility, and communication rules
- Waive (give up) or preserve claims under the *Family Property Act* and the *Family Law Act*
- Be filed as a consent order (an order both sides agree to), so it can be enforced as a court order

What a separation agreement cannot do:

- Grant a divorce or undo the marriage
- Limit the court's power to vary child support, which can always be changed based on the child's best interests and the payor's income
- Override the best interests of the child test if a parenting term is later challenged

A properly drafted agreement settles nearly everything people fight about in family court, without the court. But the marriage stays in place as a legal status until someone applies for divorce.

## When People Delay the Divorce, and When They Should Not

Many people separate, sign an agreement, and never apply for a divorce. That can work for years, but sometimes the delay causes real problems.

**Remarriage.** You cannot legally marry someone else in Canada until your current marriage is ended by divorce or annulment. If a new relationship is heading toward marriage, the divorce needs to be filed.

**Property limitation periods.** Under s. 6 of the *Family Property Act*, a married spouse has two years from the date of the divorce order to apply for division of family property. For AIPs, the two-year period runs from the date the applicant knew or ought to have known the relationship had ended. For a married spouse, delaying the divorce keeps the claim open. Once the divorce is granted, though, the two years start to run, and missing that deadline usually ends the claim.

**Estate and pension planning.** Separation by itself does not revoke (cancel) a will, change a pension beneficiary, or remove a spouse from a life insurance policy. Until the divorce is granted and designations are updated, your separated spouse may still inherit as your named beneficiary or receive pension survivor benefits. Review your will, enduring power of attorney, personal directive, and beneficiary designations as soon as you separate.

**Name changes.** You do not need a court order or a divorce to return to a former surname in Alberta. Apply through Vital Statistics using the legal change of name process.

**Tax, benefits, and immigration.** Marital status affects tax returns, GST credits, Canada Child Benefit calculations, and spousal sponsorship applications. Mistakes lead to reassessments and demands to repay overpaid benefits.
