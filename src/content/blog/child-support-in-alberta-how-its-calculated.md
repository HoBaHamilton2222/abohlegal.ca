Child support in Alberta is more mechanical than most people expect. Under both the federal and Alberta guidelines, the paying parent's income, the number of children, and the province of residence drive a table amount. That is the starting point for what the other parent receives each month, and the rest is mostly adjustment.

## Where the Numbers Come From

Two statutes and two sets of guidelines govern child support in Alberta. If you are divorcing, the federal *Divorce Act*, RSC 1985, c 3 (2nd Supp), applies. The amount is set by the *Federal Child Support Guidelines*, SOR/97-175, a regulation made under section 26.1 of that Act.

If you are unmarried, or separated but not divorcing, Alberta's *Family Law Act*, SA 2003, c F-4.5, applies. Section 51 of that Act directs the court to make a child support order in accordance with the prescribed guidelines, the *Alberta Child Support Guidelines*, Alta Reg 147/2005. Both sets of guidelines mirror each other on the core calculation, so the answer is largely the same either way.

You do not add both parents' incomes together for the base calculation. The receiving parent's income matters for special expenses and shared parenting, but not for the basic table number.

## Finding the Table Amount

The *Federal Child Support Tables* set out a monthly amount for every income level and number of children, by province. Alberta has its own table because the amounts are built on provincial income tax rates.

The tables were updated effective October 1, 2025, under SOR/2025-166. If your order was made before October 1, 2025, the 2017 tables still determine support up to that date, and the 2025 tables apply going forward. A change in the table amount can be a material change in circumstances (an important change since the order) justifying a variation. But the switch is not automatic.

Justice Canada's [2025 Child Support Table Look-up](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/fl-df/child-enfant/2025/look-rech.aspx) gives you the monthly base amount. Enter the paying parent's gross annual income (income before tax), the province, and the number of children.

## A Worked Example

Jordan and Sam separated last year. Both live in Alberta, and their two children, ages 8 and 11, live primarily with Sam. Jordan earns $80,000 gross from employment, and Sam works part-time and earns $35,000.

Step one is the table amount. Using the 2025 *Federal Child Support Tables* for Alberta, two children, at a paying-parent income of $80,000, the basic monthly amount is **$1,166**. That is what Jordan owes Sam each month as base child support, before any special expenses. We did not subtract Sam's income from Jordan's or average the two. Sam's income matters only for section 7 expenses (special expenses, covered below).

Step two adds any section 7 expenses, such as daycare, major extracurriculars, or significant uncovered health costs, on top of the $1,166. Step three is the parenting arrangement. If Jordan has the children less than 40 percent of the time, the table amount stands, but at 40 percent or more the calculation changes.

## Section 7 Expenses (What Is Added On Top)

Section 7 of the *Federal Child Support Guidelines* allows the court to order contributions toward "special or extraordinary expenses" beyond the table amount. The list in section 7(1) is closed (only these count):

- Child care expenses necessary because the parent with majority parenting time is working, ill, disabled, or in school
- The portion of medical and dental insurance premiums attributable to the child
- Health-related expenses exceeding insurance reimbursement by at least $100 annually (orthodontics, counselling, physiotherapy, prescription drugs, hearing aids, glasses)
- Extraordinary primary or secondary school expenses
- Post-secondary education expenses
- Extraordinary extracurricular expenses

The expense must be necessary for the child and reasonable in relation to the parents' means (income and resources). A weekend soccer league is usually not a section 7 expense, but competitive hockey with travel, tournament fees, and equipment often is.

Once an expense qualifies, the cost is shared between the parents in proportion to their incomes, after deducting any subsidies, tax credits, or benefits that offset it.

Back to Jordan and Sam: suppose after-school care costs $800 per month and the full amount qualifies. Their combined income is $115,000, so Jordan's share is $80,000 divided by $115,000, or about 69.6 percent. Jordan's contribution is roughly $557 per month on top of the $1,166 table amount, for total monthly child support of $1,723.

## Shared Parenting Changes Everything

If each parent has the children for at least 40 percent of parenting time over the course of a year, section 9 of the *Federal Child Support Guidelines* applies. The ordinary table amount no longer controls. Under section 9, the court considers:

- The table amount each parent would owe if the other had majority parenting time
- The increased costs of shared parenting
- The conditions, means, needs, and circumstances of each parent and child

In practice, the starting point is a "set-off." You subtract the lower table amount from the higher, and the higher-earning parent pays the difference.

If Jordan and Sam shared time equally, Jordan's table amount at $80,000 is $1,166 and Sam's at $35,000 is $499. The set-off is $667 per month payable by Jordan to Sam.

Set-off is only the starting point. In *Contino v. Leonelli-Contino*, 2005 SCC 63, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that judges in shared parenting cases have broad discretion to depart from the set-off figure. They can do so to make sure the children enjoy a comparable standard of living in each home.

The 40 percent threshold is about time actually exercised. Courts look at what happens in practice, not at the paper schedule.

## When Income Is Not What They Say It Is

Section 19 of the *Federal Child Support Guidelines* lets a court impute income (assign an income figure) where a parent's reported income does not reflect their real earning capacity. Listed circumstances include intentional under-employment or unemployment, diverting income, failing to produce income information, unreasonably deducting expenses, or living off sources taxed at lower rates. Self-employed payors (parents who pay support) are often the subject of imputation, because business write-offs can understate real cash flow.

Section 10, undue hardship, works the other way. Either parent can ask the court for an amount different from the table amount if the parent asking, or a child, would otherwise suffer undue hardship, for example because of unusually high debts from the relationship or unusually high costs of parenting time. The test is hard to meet. The court must refuse the request if the household of the parent claiming hardship would still have a higher standard of living than the other parent's household. Most undue hardship claims fail.

## What Not to Do

Do not pay support directly to the child. Money handed to a teenager is a gift and does not discharge (satisfy or pay off) your obligation to the other parent.

Do not stop paying because parenting time is being blocked. Child support and parenting time are legally separate issues, and withholding support for that reason is a breach, not a remedy. Bring a parenting enforcement application instead, and keep paying.

Do not rely on informal side deals. A private agreement to pay $400 a month instead of the table amount is not enforceable against the child's right to support. It can come back years later as retroactive arrears (support owed for past periods).
