A personal directive names someone you trust to make health care and personal decisions for you, following your wishes, if you cannot make them yourself. Without one, your family may face a slow, costly court process before anyone has legal authority to decide about your care.

## What Is a Personal Directive?

Under Alberta's *Personal Directives Act* (RSA 2000, c P-6), a personal directive is a legal document that lets you name an agent, sometimes called a decision-maker. Your agent can make personal, non-financial decisions for you if you lose capacity (the mental ability to make your own decisions). These include:

- **Health care decisions.** Consenting to or refusing medical treatments, surgeries, and procedures. Decisions about palliative care (care focused on comfort, not cure), resuscitation, and life-sustaining treatment.
- **Residential decisions.** Where you live, such as your own home, a care facility, or with family.
- **Day-to-day personal matters.** Diet, daily activities, social life, and other parts of daily life.

A personal directive does not cover financial or property decisions. Those need a separate document, an enduring power of attorney, under Alberta's *Powers of Attorney Act* (RSA 2000, c P-20). Every adult Albertan should have both.

## What You Can Include

You can give your agent specific instructions that they must follow. For example, you can set out your wishes on:

- Whether you want life-sustaining treatment in certain situations
- Organ and tissue donation
- Specific medical treatments you consent to or refuse
- Where you want to live
- The values and beliefs that should guide your agent's decisions

The more specific you are, the easier it is for your agent to honour your wishes.

## Why It Matters: The Alternative Is Expensive and Slow

If you lose capacity without a personal directive, your family has to go to court under the *Adult Guardianship and Trusteeship Act* (SA 2008, c A-4.2). They must apply for a guardianship order (a court order appointing a decision-maker for you). The process involves:

- A formal capacity assessment, which costs up to $500 for guardianship alone or $700 for a combined guardianship and trusteeship assessment
- Court filing fees of $300
- Criminal record and reference checks for the applicant
- A submission through the Office of the Public Guardian and Trustee (OPGT)
- A court hearing before a judge

Government fees alone run $800 to $1,000 or more. With legal fees, a simple application can cost several thousand dollars, and a contested or complex one costs more. It can also be months before a guardianship order is granted, and until then your family may not have the legal authority to make urgent decisions about your care.

A personal directive avoids all of that. It gives your chosen agent immediate authority to step in when needed, without going to court.

## Who Should Have One?

Every adult Albertan. Under the *Personal Directives Act*, any person aged 18 or older with mental capacity can make a personal directive.

Incapacity is not just a risk for older people. A 35-year-old with a serious head injury from a car crash needs someone with legal authority to make medical decisions just as much as an 85-year-old with advanced dementia.

## What Makes a Personal Directive Valid in Alberta?

The formal requirements under section 5 of the *Personal Directives Act* are simple, but you must follow them exactly:

- The directive must be in writing and dated
- It must be signed at the end by the person making it, in the presence of one witness
- The witness must sign the directive in the presence of the person making it

Your agent, your agent's spouse or adult interdependent partner (similar to a common-law partner), and your own spouse or adult interdependent partner cannot witness the directive.

If the person making the directive cannot physically sign, another person may sign on their behalf in the presence of both the person making the directive and a witness.

Alberta has also permanently adopted remote execution (signing and witnessing over video) for personal directives. It began as a temporary measure during COVID-19. The law now permits it as long as a Law Society of Alberta member lawyer is present for the signing and witnessing. That lawyer must also have provided legal advice on the documents. This helps rural Albertans who live far from a lawyer's office.

## Can You Change or Revoke a Personal Directive?

Yes. As long as you still have mental capacity, you can revoke your personal directive (cancel it formally) or amend it (make changes to it) at any time. Under section 8 of the Act, you can revoke it by:

- Creating a new personal directive that says it revokes the earlier one. A new directive that does not say so revokes the earlier one only to the extent the two contradict each other. That is because the Act allows you to have more than one directive
- Signing a written revocation statement
- Physically destroying the original directive
- Including a date or triggering event in the directive itself that ends it

An amendment follows the same formal requirements as the original directive. It must be in writing, signed, and witnessed.

## Can You Name More Than One Agent?

Yes. You can name several agents, plus alternate agents. If your main agent is unwilling or unable to act when the time comes, the alternate steps in. You can also say whether co-agents must act jointly (both must agree) or can act independently.

## The OPGT and the Personal Directives Registry

Alberta's Office of the Public Guardian and Trustee keeps a Personal Directives Registry. Registration is voluntary, but it can help: authorized health professionals and emergency responders can access the registry to confirm your agent's contact details in an emergency.

The OPGT also receives complaints about how agents act, investigates concerns, and can take action to protect vulnerable adults. If an adult lacks capacity and has no agent or guardian, the OPGT may step in as guardian of last resort (when no one else is available).

## How to Get Started

You must make a personal directive while you have the mental capacity to understand its purpose and effect. Do not wait for a health crisis, because by then it may be too late.
