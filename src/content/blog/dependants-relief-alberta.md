People say it all the time. "It is my property. I can leave it to whoever I want." That belief runs deep. You earned it. You built it. You should decide where it goes when you die.

In Alberta, that is only half true.

The law starts with testamentary freedom. You can disinherit a child. You can leave your entire estate to a new partner, a favourite charity, or the neighbour who mowed your lawn. But freedom is not absolute. If you leave someone behind who was actually depending on you, the *Wills and Succession Act*, SA 2010, c W-12.2 gives that person a separate right to go to court and ask for support out of your estate. Alberta still uses the old informal label, "dependant's relief." The Act itself calls it family maintenance and support.

This is a different animal from challenging the validity of the will. A will challenge attacks the document, arguing it should be set aside for lack of capacity, undue influence, or some other defect. Dependant's relief accepts the will as valid and asks the court to top it up. Two separate causes of action, sometimes available in the same file. If you want the will itself attacked, that is a different conversation, covered in [Challenging a Will in Alberta](/resources/challenging-a-will-in-alberta).

## The Quick Truth

If you were dependent on a person who died, and their will (or the rules of intestacy) did not make adequate provision for your maintenance, you may apply for an order carving out a share of the estate. The court has broad discretion. It weighs your needs against the size of the estate and the claims of other beneficiaries. It is not a guaranteed result. It is a claim.

## Who Can Actually Bring a Claim

Part 5 of the *Wills and Succession Act* governs family maintenance and support. Not everyone with hurt feelings about a will gets to apply. Section 87 defines a closed list of "family members," and only those persons have standing:

- The **surviving spouse** of the deceased.
- The **adult interdependent partner** of the deceased. Alberta's name for a common-law partner, as defined under the *Adult Interdependent Relationships Act*, SA 2002, c A-4.5.
- A **minor child** of the deceased (under 18).
- An **adult child aged 18 to 22** who is unable to withdraw from parental charge because they are a full-time student, mirroring the *Family Law Act*, SA 2003, c F-4.5.
- A **child of any age unable to earn a livelihood** by reason of mental or physical disability. No upper age cap. A 55-year-old disabled adult child applies on the same footing as a minor.
- A **minor grandchild or great-grandchild** who was being cared for or supported by the deceased at the time of death.

Notice who is not on that list. Financially independent adult children. Siblings. Parents of the deceased. Close friends. Former spouses. An adult child who was simply written out of the will and is angry about it has no standing under Part 5 on that basis alone. That person's only route is to challenge the will's validity, which is a much harder fight.

One quiet gap. A child to whom the deceased stood in the place of a parent but never adopted is not on the Part 5 list. The Alberta Law Reform Institute has recommended adding that category. The legislature has not. If you are in that situation, get advice early.

## What the Court Weighs

If you clear the standing hurdle, the next question is what the court actually does with the file. Section 93 of the *Wills and Succession Act* sets out the factors the court must consider. In plain English:

- **The nature and duration of the relationship** with the deceased.
- **The age and health** of the applicant.
- The applicant's **capacity to contribute to their own support**. If you are able-bodied, employed, and financially comfortable, that cuts against you.
- Any **legal obligation** the deceased had to support the applicant, and the extent of it.
- The **deceased's reasons** for making or not making provision, as expressed in the will or other evidence. A carefully explained disinheritance weighs differently than silence.
- The **size, nature, and distribution** of the estate.
- **Other people** the deceased had a legal or moral obligation to support, and what they are already receiving.
- **The conduct** of the applicant toward the deceased.

The animating idea behind this framework comes from the Supreme Court of Canada in *Tataryn v. Tataryn Estate*, 1994 CanLII 51 (SCC), [1994] 2 SCR 807. The court identified two kinds of obligations a testator has: legal obligations, tracking what the law would have required had the testator lived, and moral obligations, reflecting society's reasonable expectations of what a decent person would do. Alberta's statute codifies factors, but *Tataryn* is still the lens. When the *Wills and Succession Act* replaced the old *Dependants Relief Act*, the province said it did not intend to change the *Tataryn* framework.

## The Clock Starts at the Grant of Probate

This is where a lot of claims die quietly.

Section 89 of the *Wills and Succession Act* gives you **six months from the date the grant of probate or administration is issued** to commence your application. Not six months from the death. Not six months from when you found out. Six months from the grant.

The court can extend that window under section 89(2), but only on its own terms, and only in respect of estate assets that have not yet been distributed. Once a personal representative has distributed the estate in good faith after the six months have expired, those assets are usually gone from the pot, and recovery becomes much harder.

If you think you may have a claim, ask the personal representative or their lawyer for a copy of the grant as soon as one issues, and get legal advice fast. Start the clock in your own head the day the grant is issued.

The application is filed in the Court of King's Bench. Section 95 of the Act gives family members a right to financial disclosure from the personal representative without needing a court order, which mirrors the disclosure culture in family law and is useful early in a claim.

## What a Dependant's Relief Award Actually Looks Like

Section 88 is the operative provision. If the court is satisfied that the deceased failed to make adequate provision for the proper maintenance and support of a family member, it may order provision be made out of the estate.

The tools are flexible by design. The court can order:

- A **lump sum** paid out of the estate.
- **Periodic payments** over a defined period.
- A **charge** on specific estate assets to secure payment.
- A **transfer** of specific property to the applicant.
- Some combination of the above.

The award comes out of the estate before the beneficiaries take their final distributions. That is why prompt filing matters. Once assets are in the beneficiaries' hands, the court's ability to claw them back is limited.

Award size varies wildly with the facts. A short, childless second marriage with a modest estate is not the same file as a thirty-year marriage where the surviving spouse was out of the workforce raising the couple's children and the will leaves everything to adult children from a first marriage.

## If You Think You Have a Claim

Two things matter most early on. First, the calendar. Note the grant date and work backward from the six-month deadline. Second, the paper. Get a copy of the will and the grant. Start a file with anything that shows your relationship to the deceased and the financial life you shared. Tax returns, bank statements, medical records if disability is in play, and any correspondence about your future.

A dependant's relief claim is a real lawsuit. It carries cost and emotional consequences. But when the facts are strong, the Act gives you a meaningful tool. A single consultation is usually enough to tell you whether the claim is viable.
