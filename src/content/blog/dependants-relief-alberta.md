In Alberta, you can leave your property to anyone you want, and you can leave nothing to a child. But the *Wills and Succession Act*, SA 2010, c W-12.2 gives a person who depended on you a separate right to ask the court for support from your estate. People still call this "dependant's relief," an old, informal name, but the Act calls it family maintenance and support.

A will challenge asks the court to set the will aside for lack of capacity (mental ability to make a will), undue influence (improper pressure), or another defect. Dependant's relief is different: it accepts the will as valid and asks the court for more support from the estate. Sometimes both claims are available in the same case (see [Challenging a Will in Alberta](/resources/challenging-a-will-in-alberta)).

## The Quick Truth

You may apply for part of an estate if two things are true. You depended on the person who died, and their will, or the rules of intestacy (used when there is no valid will), did not make adequate provision for your maintenance. The court has broad discretion (freedom to decide) and weighs your needs against the size of the estate and other beneficiaries' claims. The result is not guaranteed.

## Who Can Actually Bring a Claim

Under Part 5 of the Act, section 72(b) sets a closed list of "family members," and only they have standing (the right to bring a claim):

- The **surviving spouse** of the deceased (the person who died).
- The **adult interdependent partner** of the deceased (Alberta's name for a common-law partner), as defined in the *Adult Interdependent Relationships Act*, SA 2002, c A-4.5.
- A **minor child** of the deceased (under 18).
- An **adult child at least 18 but under 22** who is unable to withdraw from parental charge (leave the parent's care) because they are a full-time student. This follows the *Family Law Act*, SA 2003, c F-4.5.
- A **child of any age unable to earn a livelihood** because of mental or physical disability. There is no upper age limit, so a disabled child of 55 can apply just as a minor can.
- A **grandchild or great-grandchild under 18** for whom the deceased stood in the place of a parent at the time of death. Under section 73(2), that means the deceased showed a settled intention to treat the grandchild as their own child. And since the grandchild's birth or for at least 2 years before the death:
    - the grandchild's primary home was with the deceased, and
    - the deceased provided the grandchild's primary financial support.

Financially independent adult children, siblings, parents of the deceased, close friends, and former spouses are not on the list. An adult child simply left out of the will has no standing under Part 5 on that basis alone. Their only route is to challenge the will's validity, which is much harder.

A child to whom the deceased stood in the place of a parent, but never adopted, is not on the list either. The Alberta Law Reform Institute has recommended adding this group, but the legislature has not.

## What the Court Weighs

Section 93 sets out the factors the court must consider. In plain English:

- **The nature and length of the relationship** with the deceased.
- **The age and health** of the applicant (the person making the claim).
- The applicant's **capacity to contribute to their own support**, including any right to support from someone else. If you are able-bodied, employed, and financially comfortable, that counts against you.
- Any **legal obligation** the deceased or the estate had to support the applicant or any other family member.
- The **deceased's reasons** for making or not making provision, as expressed in the will or other evidence, including any written statement the deceased signed about it. A carefully explained disinheritance is weighed differently from silence.
- Any **agreement or waiver** (giving up a legal right) between the deceased and the applicant that is relevant to the claim.
- The **size, nature, and distribution** of the estate, and any property or benefit the applicant or anyone else is entitled to receive because of the death.
- **Property the deceased put in trust or transferred** to someone during their lifetime, whether as a gift, under an agreement or order, or otherwise.
- Property or benefits anyone is entitled to because of the death under the *Family Property Act*, the *Dower Act*, or the family home rules in Division 1 of Part 5.

The court may also consider anything else it finds relevant.

Moral obligation is not one of the section 93 factors. Separately, courts have also considered moral claims. They have drawn on the Supreme Court of Canada's decision in *Tataryn v. Tataryn Estate*, 1994 CanLII 51 (SCC), [1994] 2 SCR 807.

In that case, the court identified two kinds of obligations a testator (a person who makes a will) has. Legal obligations match what the law would have required if the testator had lived. Moral obligations reflect society's reasonable expectations of what a decent person would do. How much weight a moral claim carries in an Alberta case depends on the case law and the facts.

The *Wills and Succession Act* replaced the old *Dependants Relief Act* for deaths on or after February 1, 2012.

## The Clock Starts at the Grant of Probate

Under section 89 of the Act, you have **six months from the date the grant of probate or administration is issued** to start your application. The time runs from the grant (the court's approval to manage the estate), not from the death or when you found out.

The court can extend the time under section 89(2), but only on its own terms and only for undistributed estate assets. If the personal representative (the executor or administrator) has distributed the estate in good faith after the six months, those assets are usually gone, and recovery becomes much harder.

The application is filed in the Court of King's Bench. Once an application has been made, section 95 of the Act lets a family member send the personal representative a written request for the financial information the regulations require. The personal representative must provide it without a court order.

## What a Dependant's Relief Award Actually Looks Like

Under section 88, the court looks at whether the deceased failed to make adequate provision for the proper maintenance and support of a family member. If it is satisfied of this, it may order that provision be made out of the estate. It can order:

- A **lump sum** paid out of the estate.
- **Periodic payments** over a set period.
- A **charge** (a legal claim against property) on specific estate assets to secure payment.
- A **transfer** of specific property to the applicant.
- A combination of these.

The award comes out of the estate before the beneficiaries receive their final shares. Once assets are in the beneficiaries' hands, the court's ability to get them back is limited, so file promptly. Award size varies widely with the facts.

## If You Think You Have a Claim

Get a copy of the will. Ask the personal representative or their lawyer for the grant as soon as it issues, then get legal advice quickly. Keep records of your relationship and shared finances, such as tax returns, bank statements, and medical records if disability is an issue.
