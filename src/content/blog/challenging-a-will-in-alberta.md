If a will does not seem to match the person who made it, Alberta law gives you ways to challenge it. The *Wills and Succession Act*, SA 2010, c W-12.2 sets out how wills are made and when a court can set one aside.

## The Will Has to Be Properly Made in the First Place

A will must first be properly executed (made with the legal formalities). Section 14 of the *Wills and Succession Act* requires the will to be in writing and signed by the testator (the person making the will). Section 15 requires two witnesses to be present together, to see the testator sign, and to sign it themselves afterward. Section 20 says those witnesses need the mental capacity to understand what they are doing.

A beneficiary (someone who receives a gift under the will), or a beneficiary's spouse or adult interdependent partner, is not disqualified from acting as a witness. But under section 21, a gift in the will to a witness, or to a witness's spouse or adult interdependent partner, is void (has no effect) unless an exception applies. One exception is where at least two other people also witnessed the signature.

Under section 40, a court can save the gift if it is satisfied of two things. First, the testator meant to make the gift despite knowing the person's role. Second, there was no improper or undue influence. That application has to be made within six months after the grant of probate or administration unless the court extends the time.

Under section 37 of the Act, a court can still validate a will that missed one of these formalities if the document plainly reflects the testator's intention. The Alberta Law Reform Institute calls this "substantial compliance." But if the execution was careless and the intent cannot be clearly shown, the court can refuse to validate the document.

A handwritten will, which section 16 calls a holograph will, does not need witnesses. It only needs to be entirely in the testator's handwriting and signed by them. Many home-made wills fail at this step: people type a document, sign it alone, and call it a will. It is not one.

## The Grounds That Actually Show Up in Court

A few grounds come up again and again in Alberta estate litigation (court disputes over estates).

**Lack of testamentary capacity.** The testator must understand what they are doing at the moment of signing. The test still used today comes from an English case, *Banks v. Goodfellow* (1870), LR 5 QB 549. The testator must:

- Understand that they are making a will
- Grasp the general extent of their property
- Recognize the people who might reasonably expect to benefit
- Not be under a delusion that distorts the disposition (how they leave their property)

Capacity also depends on the task. A person with early dementia may have been clear-headed enough one morning to make a simple will, even if they could not manage their finances by that afternoon. Evidence from the lawyer who drafted the will, the treating doctor, and close family usually decides the issue.

**Undue influence.** This is not persuasion or some pressure from a relative. It is coercion that overrides the testator's own free will, so the document reflects the influencer's wishes instead. Direct evidence is rare. Courts look at the circumstances: who was isolating the testator, who drove them to the lawyer, and whether they had any independent advice.

**Knowledge and approval.** Even a testator with full capacity must know what is in their will and agree to it. This ground can apply if they could not read the will's language, the signing was rushed, or whole clauses were skipped when it was read to them.

**Fraud or forgery.** This covers things like a swapped page, a faked signature, or a testator misled about what they were signing.

**Revocation.** A later valid will replaces an earlier one. A will can also be revoked (cancelled) by destroying it with the intent to revoke it. If a more recent document is found, that changes everything.

## "Suspicious Circumstances" Change Who Has to Prove What

Usually, the person challenging a will carries the burden of proof (the duty to prove the claim). If you claim the testator lacked capacity, you have to show it. But in *Vout v. Hay*, [1995] 2 SCR 876, the Supreme Court of Canada set out a different rule when "suspicious circumstances" surround the making of the will. Examples include:

- A brand new will signed days before death
- A dramatic change from every earlier will
- A beneficiary who arranged the meeting with the lawyer
- A testator in sharp mental decline

In those situations, the presumption of knowledge and approval (the court's usual starting assumption) no longer applies. The burden shifts to the person propounding the will (asking the court to accept it) to show that it really is valid. In Alberta estate litigation, suspicious circumstances that are well pleaded (clearly set out in the court documents) often decide whether a claim moves forward.

## How You Actually Challenge a Will

In Alberta, the formal process starts with a caveat. The Surrogate Rules, Alta Reg 130/1995, govern the probate process (how a court approves a will). A caveat is a written notice filed with the Court of King's Bench telling the court that you oppose the grant of probate. Caveats still have to be filed on paper, even though the rest of probate has moved to the Surrogate Digital Service platform.

Once a caveat is filed, the probate application is stopped. A caveat stays in force for three months from the date it is filed unless the court orders otherwise. This rule is in section 47 of the *Estate Administration Act*, SA 2014, c E-12.5.

The person applying for probate can also serve you with a warning (Form C4). Once you are served with that warning, you have only 10 days to file a notice of objection (Form C9) setting out your grounds and serve it on the applicant. You can apply to the court to extend that time.

If you miss that deadline, the caveat is discharged (cancelled) and the application goes ahead as if nothing happened. If you file the notice of objection in time, the case becomes contentious: in plain terms, a full lawsuit is now underway.

From there, it is a regular Court of King's Bench action. The steps include:

- Pleadings (the written claims and responses)
- Disclosure (sharing relevant records)
- Questioning (answering questions under oath)
- Affidavit evidence (sworn written statements)
- Expert reports, where relevant
- Rarely, a trial

Most estate cases settle long before trial, because the costs rise quickly and the process is hard on everyone.

## A Word Before You File Anything

A will challenge is expensive and slow, and it strains family relationships already hurt by grief. Courts take costs seriously: a challenger who loses can be ordered to pay part of the other side's legal fees. If the grounds are weak, sometimes the right choice is not to go ahead.

But when the facts are strong, the law gives you real tools. The sooner a lawyer reviews the medical records, the drafting lawyer's file, and the history of earlier wills, the stronger your position.
